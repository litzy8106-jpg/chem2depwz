CHEM 18 · Thermodynamics & chemical equilibrium

Where the energy goes, and which way things run

Thermodynamics answers two questions. The first law keeps the books: energy moves between system and surroundings as heat and work, but the total never changes. The second law decides direction: a process runs on its own only if it increases the entropy of the universe. Gibbs free energy combines both into one number you can compute. Chemical equilibrium is where the two meet: ΔG° = −RT ln K tells you how far a reaction goes before it stops, and Le Châtelier tells you which way it moves when you disturb it.

Lesson 1

Systems, surroundings, and state functions

Chemical thermodynamics is the study of energy change, transformation and exchange. Before you can track energy, you have to decide what you are tracking it for.

What
  • System: the part of the universe under investigation, separated from the rest by a real or imaginary boundary.
  • Surroundings: the rest of the universe. System + surroundings = universe.
Three types of system
TypeExchanges matter?Exchanges energy?Example
OpenYesYesAn uncovered beaker of boiling water; your body
ClosedNoYesA stoppered flask; a sealed piston
IsolatedNoNoAn ideal thermos or a sealed bomb calorimeter (approximately)
  • Extensive properties depend on the amount: moles, mass, volume, energy, enthalpy.
  • Intensive properties do not: density, temperature, melting point, boiling point, solubility.
  • State functions depend only on the initial and final states: P, V, T, E (or U), H, S, G.
  • Path (nonstate) functions depend on how the change happened: work (w) and heat (q).
How

In the handout's example, Zn(s) and HCl(aq) react in an open flask. The system is the reacting mixture in the flask. The flask, the air and the thermometer are the surroundings. Because H2 gas escapes and heat leaves, it is an open system. Stopper the flask and it becomes closed.

The letter clue: state functions are usually written with capitals (E, H, S, G, P, V, T); path functions with lowercase (q, w). So in ΔE = q + w, a state function (ΔE) equals the sum of two path functions.

Why

The handout's mountain picture says it best: the change in altitude between the base and the summit (4004 m) is the same whether you take the winding road or the helicopter. That is a state function. The distance traveled (5662 m vs 9857 m) depends on the route. That is a path function.

This matters because it lets you calculate ΔH or ΔE by any convenient route, including routes that never happen in real life (Hess's law, Born–Haber cycles). You can never do that with q or w alone: the same overall change can release different amounts of heat and work depending on how it is carried out. Only their sum, ΔE, is fixed.

Check yourself

Is the heat released when 2 mol of methane burns extensive or intensive?

Extensive. Burning twice as much releases twice the heat. ΔH per mole (kJ/mol) is the intensive version.

A reaction runs inside a sealed, perfectly insulated steel container. What type of system?

Isolated: the seal blocks matter and the insulation blocks heat.

Lesson 2

The first law of thermodynamics

What

Energy is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. It only moves between system and surroundings. The energy a system holds is its internal energy, E: the sum of all kinetic and potential energies of its particles. You cannot measure E itself, only its change.

ΔE = Efinal − Einitial = q + w    (equivalently q = ΔE + (−w))
Sign convention used in your course (chemists' convention)
QuantityPositive (+)Negative (−)
q (heat)+ heat absorbed by the system− heat released by the system
w (work)+ work done on the system− work done by the system
ΔE+ system gains energy (endothermic)− system loses energy (exothermic)
How

Translate each word into a sign before you touch numbers. "Receives," "absorbs," "is added" mean q > 0. "Loses," "releases," "gives off" mean q < 0. "Does work," "delivers work," "expands against the atmosphere" mean w < 0. "Is compressed," "work is done on it" mean w > 0.

Handout Question 1: a system receives 575 J of heat and delivers 875 J of work. Find ΔE.

q = +575 J (received). w = −875 J (delivered by the system).

ΔE = 575 + (−875) = −300 J. The system gave out more energy as work than it took in as heat, so its internal energy fell.

Handout Question 2: expanding gases do 450 J of work on pistons and the engine loses 330 J as heat. Find ΔE in kcal.

System = the burning gasoline and its products. q = −330 J (lost). w = −450 J (done by the gases).

ΔE = −330 + (−450) = −780 J. Convert: −780 J × (1 kJ/1000 J) × (1 kcal/4.184 kJ) = −0.186 kcal.

Two special cases from your handout:

  • No work (w = 0): ΔE = q. Ice melting in a glass absorbs heat, so q > 0 and ΔE > 0 (endothermic). Water freezing releases heat: q < 0, ΔE < 0 (exothermic).
  • No heat (q = 0), an adiabatic process: ΔE = w. When Zn + HCl makes H2 that pushes a piston in an insulated tank, all the energy leaves as work and ΔE < 0.
Why

Because energy is conserved, whatever the system loses the surroundings must gain, exactly: ΔEsystem = −ΔEsurroundings. The handout's twin bar graphs show this: when the system's bar grows, the surroundings' bar shrinks by the same amount. The signs are written from the system's point of view, which is why "work done by the system" is negative: energy left the system.

Heat and work are the only two ways energy crosses the boundary of a closed system. Heat flows because of a temperature difference; work is energy transferred when something is moved by a force (like a piston). That is why their sum accounts for the entire change in E.

Lesson 3

Work and the four types of process

What

Most chemical work is pressure–volume (PΔV) work: a gas pushing back the atmosphere or a piston as it expands.

w = −PextΔV    (irreversible, against a constant external pressure)

Pext is the outside pressure, not the gas pressure inside. With P in atm and V in L, w comes out in L·atm; multiply by 101.3 J/L·atm (or by 8.314/0.08205) to get joules.

wrev = −nRT ln(V2/V1)    (reversible, isothermal, ideal gas)
How
Four ways a gas can change (ideal gas)
ProcessHeld constantKey results
IsobaricPressureqp = nCpΔT = ΔH; w = −PΔV = −nRΔT
IsochoricVolumew = 0, so qv = ΔE = nCvΔT
IsothermalTemperatureΔE = 0 and ΔH = 0 for an ideal gas, so q = −w
AdiabaticNo heat flow (q = 0)ΔE = w: expansion work is paid for entirely from internal energy, so the gas cools
Handout example: 1 mol ideal gas at 2 atm expands from 10.0 L to 14.0 L. Work if (a) irreversible against 1 atm, (b) reversible?

(a) w = −(1 atm)(14.0 − 10.0 L) = −4.0 L·atm × 101.3 J/L·atm = −405.3 J.

(b) T = PV/nR = (2 atm)(10.0 L)/[(1 mol)(0.08205)] = 243.8 K. w = −(1)(8.314)(243.8) ln(14.0/10.0) = −682 J.

The reversible path does about 1.7 times more work for the same expansion.

From isobaric heating the handout derives Cp = Cv + R for an ideal gas: at constant pressure the heat must both raise the temperature (nCvΔT) and pay for expansion work (nRΔT).

Why

Why reversible work is the maximum. In a reversible process Pext is kept equal to Pgas at every instant, so the gas always pushes against the largest pressure it can still overcome. In an irreversible expansion it pushes against a smaller, fixed Pext and the "extra" push is wasted. On a P–V graph, work is the area under the path; the reversible curve encloses more area than the flat line at Pext. (Lab 2 in Remember draws this.)

Why ΔE = 0 for an isothermal ideal gas. An ideal gas has no intermolecular forces, so its internal energy is only the kinetic energy of its molecules, which depends only on T. Same T, same E. Any heat absorbed therefore leaves immediately as work.

Lesson 4

Enthalpy, H

What

Enthalpy is the thermodynamic variable equal to the heat gained or lost in a constant-pressure process. Most reactions in open beakers, and in your body, happen at constant pressure, which is why ΔH is the energy change you see in tables.

ΔH = ΔE + Δ(PV)    at constant P: ΔH = ΔE + PΔV = qp
For gases: Δ(PV) = ΔngRT, so ΔH = ΔE + ΔngRT

Δng = moles of gaseous products − moles of gaseous reactants. Solids and liquids are ignored because their volumes are tiny.

  • ΔH < 0: exothermic, heat released to the surroundings.
  • ΔH > 0: endothermic, heat absorbed from the surroundings.
How
  1. Count Δng from the balanced equation (gases only).
  2. Use R in units that match: 8.314 × 10−3 kJ/mol·K for kJ, or 1.987 cal/mol·K for cal.
  3. ΔH = ΔE + ΔngRT. If Δng = 0, then ΔH = ΔE.
Past quiz: B2H6(g) + 3O2(g) → B2O3(s) + 3H2O(l), ΔE° = −2143.2 kJ. Find ΔH°.

Gases: reactants 1 + 3 = 4 mol; products 0 (solid and liquid). Δng = 0 − 4 = −4.

ΔH = −2143.2 kJ + (−4)(8.314 × 10−3)(298) = −2143.2 − 9.9 = −2153.1 kJ.

ΔH scales with the equation: double the coefficients, double ΔH. Reverse the reaction, flip the sign. Propane combustion releases 2043.0 kJ per mole of propane no matter what path it takes.

Why

At constant pressure, some of the energy change goes into PΔV work instead of heat. Enthalpy adds the PV term back in, so that qp = ΔH exactly. That is why a coffee-cup calorimeter measures ΔH while a sealed bomb calorimeter (no volume change, w = 0) measures ΔE.

When gas moles decrease (Δng < 0), the atmosphere does work on the shrinking system, so more heat comes out at constant pressure than at constant volume: ΔH is more negative than ΔE, as in the B2H6 problem. The correction is small (RT ≈ 2.5 kJ per mole of gas at 25 °C) but exams test the sign.

Lesson 5

Calorimetry

What

A calorimeter measures heat flow from the temperature change of something with known heat capacity.

q = CcalΔT (calorimeter constant, kJ/°C)  ·  q = m·c·ΔT (specific heat)  ·  q = n·Cm·ΔT (molar heat capacity)
qreaction = −qcalorimeter
  • Bomb calorimeter: sealed steel vessel, constant volume, w = 0, so qv = ΔE.
  • Coffee-cup (constant-pressure) calorimeter: open to the atmosphere, constant pressure, so qp = ΔH.
How
Handout Worked Example 1: 10.3 mg benzoic acid (MW 122) burns in a bomb; T rises 24.5 → 25.1 °C; Ccal = 0.22 kJ/°C. Find qv, ΔE, ΔH and qp.

qv = −(0.22 kJ/°C)(0.6 °C) = −0.132 kJ = −132 J (negative because the reaction released the heat the calorimeter absorbed).

ΔE per mole = −132 J ÷ 10.3 mg × 1000 mg/g × 122 g/mol = −1563 kJ/mol.

ΔH: C6H5COOH(s) + 15/2 O2 → 7CO2(g) + 3H2O(l); Δng = 7 − 7.5 = −0.5. ΔH = −1563 + (−0.5)(8.314 × 10−3)(298) = −1564 kJ/mol.

qp for 10.3 mg at constant pressure = −1564 kJ/mol × (10.3 × 10−3 g/122 g/mol) = −0.132 kJ. Almost the same, because Δng is small.

Handout Worked Example 2: 0.14 g CH3OH burned at constant pressure raises T by 0.222 °C; Cp(cal) = 1.5 kJ/°C. Find qp and ΔH.

qp = −(1.5)(0.222) = −0.333 kJ. Moles = 0.14/32 = 4.38 × 10−3 mol. ΔH = −0.333/4.38 × 10−3 = −76 kJ/mol.

Method check: the accepted enthalpy of combustion of methanol is about −726 kJ/mol, so the handout's sample data are best treated as a method exercise. Follow the same steps on your exam.

Why

The calorimeter and the reaction form an (approximately) isolated system together, so by the first law the heat the reaction releases is exactly the heat the calorimeter absorbs: qrxn = −qcal. The sign flip is where most errors happen. A temperature rise means the reaction is exothermic.

The bomb measures ΔE because rigid walls make ΔV = 0, so no PV work is possible and all the energy change shows up as heat. The cup measures ΔH because the pressure is fixed by the atmosphere. Convert between them with ΔngRT.

Health linkThe Calories on Philippine food labels are kilocalories measured by burning food in bomb calorimeters (then corrected for digestion). 1 food Calorie = 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ. A 2000-kcal daily intake is about 8.4 MJ.
Lesson 6 · Evaluating ΔH, method 1

Heats of formation, ΔH°f

What

The standard heat of formation is the enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states (25 °C, 1 bar). By definition, ΔH°f of a pure element in its standard state is zero (O2(g), H2(g), C(graphite), Na(s)…).

ΔH°rxn = Σ n ΔH°f(products) − Σ m ΔH°f(reactants)
How
Handout: enthalpy of combustion of C2H4(g). ΔH°f: C2H4 +12.4, CO2 −94.0, H2O(l) −68.3 kcal/mol

C2H4(g) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)

ΔH = [2(−94.0) + 2(−68.3)] − [12.4 + 3(0)] = −324.6 − 12.4 = −337 kcal.

Past quiz: ΔH°f of glucose from ΔHcomb = −2816 kJ/mol, ΔH°f CO2 = −393.5, H2O(l) = −285.9 kJ/mol

C6H12O6(s) + 6O2(g) → 6CO2(g) + 6H2O(l), ΔH = −2816 kJ.

−2816 = [6(−393.5) + 6(−285.9)] − [ΔH°f(glucose) + 0] = −4076.4 − ΔH°f.

ΔH°f(glucose) = −4076.4 + 2816 = −1260 kJ/mol. The unknown can sit on either side; just solve the same equation for it.

  • Multiply each ΔH°f by its coefficient.
  • Watch the phase: H2O(l) is −285.8 kJ/mol but H2O(g) is −241.8 kJ/mol.
  • Elements contribute zero, but do not forget to write them in.
Why

Because H is a state function, you can imagine any reaction as two steps: break all reactants down into elements (the reverse of their formation, so −ΣΔH°f reactants), then build the products from those same elements (+ΣΔH°f products). The elements are a common reference "sea level," which is exactly why their ΔH°f is set to zero. "Products minus reactants" is just Hess's law with formation reactions as the steps.

Lesson 7 · Evaluating ΔH, method 2

Hess's law

What

Hess's law (the law of heat summation): if a reaction can be written as the sum of several steps, its ΔH is the sum of the steps' ΔH values. ΔH does not depend on the route taken.

  • Reverse an equation → change the sign of its ΔH.
  • Multiply an equation by a factor → multiply its ΔH by the same factor.
  • Add the equations, cancel species that appear on both sides, and add the ΔH values.
How

Work from the target equation. For each target species, find the given equation that contains it, and flip or scale that equation so the species lands on the correct side with the correct coefficient. Species that appear in no target position must cancel out.

Handout: C2H4(g) + H2(g) → C2H6(g), from combustion data

Given: (1) C2H6 + 7/2 O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O, ΔH = −368.4 kcal; (2) C2H4 + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 2H2O, −337.2 kcal; (3) H2 + ½O2 → H2O, −68.32 kcal.

C2H6 must be a product, so reverse (1): +368.4 kcal. C2H4 and H2 are already reactants in (2) and (3), so keep them.

Add: 2CO2, 3H2O and 7/2 O2 all cancel. ΔH = +368.4 − 337.2 − 68.32 = −37.1 kcal.

Lab 3 in Remember lets you do this one (and a harder one) by clicking reverse and ×2 / ×½.

Why

Hess's law is just the state-function property of Lesson 1 applied to reactions. Enthalpy is like altitude: going from reactants to products by one big step or by three small steps ends at the same height. Reversing a step means walking downhill instead of up, so the sign flips; doing a step twice doubles the climb. This is what lets chemists find ΔH for reactions that are too slow, too dangerous or impossible to run directly, like C(graphite) + 2H2 → CH4.

Lesson 8 · Evaluating ΔH, method 3

Bond enthalpy and the Born–Haber cycle

What

Bond enthalpy is the energy required to break 1 mole of a given bond in the gas phase. It is always positive.

  • Bond breaking: ΔH = + (energy goes in).
  • Bond forming: ΔH = − (energy comes out).
ΔHrxn ≈ Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds broken) − Σ(bond enthalpies of bonds formed)

The Born–Haber cycle applies the same idea to forming an ionic solid from a metal and a nonmetal. It is used mainly to calculate lattice energy (U), which cannot be measured directly.

ΔH°f = ΔHsub + IE + ΔHdiss + EA + U
How
Handout: combustion of propane from bond enthalpies (C–H 413, C–C 347, O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 464 kJ/mol)

CH3CH2CH3 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O

Broken: 8 C–H (8 × 413) + 2 C–C (2 × 347) + 5 O=O (5 × 498) = +6488 kJ.

Formed: 6 C=O (two per CO2, 6 × 805) + 8 O–H (two per H2O, 8 × 464) = 8542 kJ released.

ΔH = 6488 − 8542 = −2054 kJ. Compare with −2043 kJ from heats of formation: close but not identical.

Handout: Born–Haber cycle for NaCl

Na(s) → Na(g): ΔHsub = +108 kJ. ½Cl2(g) → Cl(g): ΔHdiss = +122 kJ (half the Cl–Cl bond enthalpy). Na(g) → Na+(g) + e−: IE = +496 kJ. Cl(g) + e− → Cl−(g): EA = −349 kJ. Na+(g) + Cl−(g) → NaCl(s): U = −788 kJ.

ΔH°f = 108 + 496 + 122 − 349 − 788 = −411 kJ/mol. On an exam, you are usually given ΔH°f and asked for U: rearrange the same sum.

Why

A reaction is exothermic when the bonds formed are stronger overall than the bonds broken. Breaking always costs energy and forming always pays, so the sign of ΔH tells you which side has the stronger bonds.

Bond enthalpy answers are approximate because tabulated values are averages over many molecules (a C–H bond in methane is not identical to one in propane) and they assume everything is a gas. That is why −2054 kJ differs from the −2043 kJ formation-data value. When ΔH°f data are available, they give the more accurate answer.

In the Born–Haber cycle, ionization is uphill and only partly repaid by electron affinity. The huge release of lattice energy, from oppositely charged ions packing together, is what makes forming NaCl favorable overall.

Lesson 9

Heats of physical transformations

What

Phase changes happen at constant temperature (they are isothermal), yet they absorb or release heat. Going solid → liquid → gas is endothermic; the reverse is exothermic.

ΔHsub = ΔHfus + ΔHvap   (Hess's law)
ΔHfreezing = −ΔHfus · ΔHcondensation = −ΔHvap · ΔHdeposition = −ΔHsub
q = n·ΔHtransition (during the plateau)  ·  q = m·c·ΔT (while warming one phase)
How

On a heating curve, sloped segments warm a single phase (use mcΔT); flat segments are phase changes (use nΔH). Add the segments for the total heat. Lab 4 in Remember draws the water curve from −10 °C to 120 °C.

Handout Worked Example 3: vaporize 1.0 kg of water at 100 °C and 1 atm (ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol). Find q, w, ΔH, ΔE.

n = 1000 g ÷ 18 g/mol = 55.6 mol.

qp = nΔHvap = 55.6 × 40.7 = 2260 kJ. ΔT = 0, but q is not zero: the heat separates molecules rather than raising T.

w = −Pext(Vgas − Vliq) ≈ −nRT (Vliq is negligible) = −(55.6)(8.314)(373) = −172 kJ.

ΔH = qp = 2261 kJ. ΔE = q + w = 2260 − 172 = 2088 kJ.

Past quiz: enthalpy of sublimation of 100 g CO2 at 183 K, ΔHsub = 3.87 kcal/mol

n = 100 g ÷ 44.01 g/mol = 2.27 mol. ΔH = 2.27 × 3.87 = 8.80 kcal. Enthalpy is extensive: scale the molar value by the moles.

Why

During melting or boiling, every bit of added energy goes into overcoming intermolecular forces (increasing potential energy), not into making molecules move faster (kinetic energy, which is what temperature measures). So T stays flat until the whole sample has changed phase.

ΔHvap is much larger than ΔHfus (40.7 vs 6.01 kJ/mol for water) because melting only loosens the hydrogen-bond network, while boiling must separate molecules completely. That is also why steam burns are worse than boiling-water burns: condensing steam on skin releases the large ΔHvap before the water even starts cooling.

Lesson 10

Spontaneity, entropy, and the second law

What

A spontaneous process happens on its own, without continuous outside input of energy, once it is allowed to start. Examples from your handout:

  • A gas expands into an evacuated flask, but never gathers back into one flask on its own.
  • An iron nail rusts, but rust never turns back into shiny iron by itself.
  • Ice melts above 0 °C, while water freezes below 0 °C. Spontaneity can depend on temperature.

Entropy (S) measures the dispersal of energy and matter, often described as disorder or randomness. More precisely, it counts the number of microscopic arrangements (microstates, W) consistent with a state: S = k ln W.

Second law: in any spontaneous process, the entropy of the universe increases.

ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr > 0 (spontaneous)  ·  = 0 (equilibrium)  ·  < 0 (nonspontaneous)
How

Entropy increases when:

  • a substance melts, vaporizes or sublimes (Ssolid < Sliquid ≪ Sgas);
  • temperature rises (more thermal motion);
  • the number of moles, especially of gas, increases: 2H2O(l) → 2H2(g) + O2(g);
  • a solid dissolves or ionizes: NaCl(s) → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq);
  • molecules get larger or more complex (more ways to vibrate and rotate).

When gases are involved, Δng is usually the deciding factor.

Why

Spontaneous change is overwhelmingly a matter of probability. If gas molecules can occupy two flasks, there are astronomically more arrangements with them spread across both than with all of them in one. The system drifts toward the state with the most microstates simply because that state is far more likely. Nothing pushes the gas back, because that would mean wandering into a vanishingly rare arrangement.

Why can exothermic processes be spontaneous even when the system's entropy drops (like water freezing)? Heat released into the surroundings increases their entropy: ΔSsurr = −ΔHsys/T. Below 0 °C that gain outweighs the system's loss, so ΔSuniv > 0. The 1/T explains the temperature dependence: the same heat makes a bigger entropy difference to cold surroundings than to hot ones.

Spontaneous does not mean fast. Diamond turning into graphite is spontaneous at room temperature but takes geological time. Thermodynamics says whether a process can happen; kinetics says how fast.

Lesson 11

Calculating entropy changes and the third law

What
Phase change (reversible, constant T): ΔS = qrev/T = ΔHtransition/T
Reaction: ΔS°rxn = Σ n S°(products) − Σ m S°(reactants)

Third law: the entropy of a pure, perfect crystalline substance is zero at absolute zero (0 K). This gives every substance an absolute entropy S°, unlike E and H, which only have changes.

Standard state (IUPAC, 1982): 25 °C, 1 bar = 105 Pa = 0.9869 atm. Units of S°: J/mol·K. Unlike ΔH°f, the S° of an element is not zero at 25 °C.

How
Handout: ΔS for vaporizing water at 100 °C, ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol

ΔS = 4.07 × 104 J/mol ÷ 373 K = +109.1 J/mol·K. Use kelvin and convert kJ to J.

Handout: ΔS° for O2(g) + 2H2(g) → 2H2O(g). S°: O2 205.0, H2 130.6, H2O(g) 188.7 J/mol·K

ΔS° = 2(188.7) − [205.0 + 2(130.6)] = 377.4 − 466.2 = −88.8 J/K. Negative: 3 mol of gas become 2.

Handout: ΔS° for CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l). S°: CH4 186.2, O2 205.0, CO2 213.6, H2O(l) 69.9

ΔS° = [213.6 + 2(69.9)] − [186.2 + 2(205.0)] = 353.4 − 596.2 = −242.8 J/K. Strongly negative: 3 mol of gas become 1 mol of gas plus liquid.

Why

What zero entropy means (the handout's question): a perfect crystal at 0 K has every particle fixed in one position with no thermal motion. There is exactly one possible arrangement, W = 1, so S = k ln 1 = 0. Any warmth or imperfection adds arrangements and raises S. The handout's S-versus-T graph shows S rising steadily within each phase and jumping at melting and boiling, where positional freedom suddenly increases. The jump at boiling is far larger.

Why ΔS = qrev/T: adding heat to a cold system creates relatively more new microstates than adding the same heat to a hot, already disordered system. Dividing by T captures that.

Why

ΔG is the second law expressed in terms of the system alone. Start from ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr, with ΔSsurr = −ΔH/T. Multiply by −T and you get −TΔSuniv = ΔH − TΔS = ΔG. So ΔG < 0 is exactly the same statement as ΔSuniv > 0, but you only need data about the system.

The equation is a tug-of-war: ΔH (the drive toward lower energy) against TΔS (the drive toward more disorder). Temperature is the referee's weight on the entropy side, which is why the entropy term wins at high T. ΔG says nothing about rate: a free-energy diagram can show products far downhill behind a high activation hill.

Lesson 13 · Chemical equilibrium

Reversible reactions and dynamic equilibrium

What

Many reactions go to completion, stopping when one reactant runs out. Others do not, because the products can react to re-form the reactants. A reversible reaction runs in both the forward (left → right) and reverse (right → left) directions, shown by a double arrow ⇌.

H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g)   ·   N2O4(g) ⇌ 2NO2(g)

Chemical equilibrium is reached when the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction. Concentrations then stop changing, but both reactions keep going. That is why it is called a dynamic equilibrium.

How

Your handout's characteristics of a system at equilibrium:

  • It is a dynamic process: forward and reverse reactions continue indefinitely.
  • It can only be reached in a closed system (nothing added or removed). In an open flask, escaping gas would keep pulling the reaction one way.
  • The final equilibrium position is the same whether you start from reactants or products. Pure N2O4 or pure NO2, the system ends in the same mixture of both (at the same T and total composition).
  • The macroscopic properties (color, pressure, concentrations) stay constant.

In the handout's graph, [N2O4] falls and [NO2] rises until they level off at 0.75 M and 0.50 M. The flat lines mark equilibrium.

Why

At the start, there is lots of N2O4 and no NO2, so the forward rate (kf[N2O4]) is high and the reverse rate (kr[NO2]2) is zero. As N2O4 is used up, the forward rate drops; as NO2 builds up, the reverse rate rises. They must meet. When they do, every N2O4 that splits is matched by two NO2 that recombine, so nothing changes overall.

Setting ratef = rater gives kf[N2O4] = kr[NO2]2, so kf/kr = [NO2]2/[N2O4] = K. The equilibrium constant is a ratio of two rate constants, which is why it depends only on temperature.

Common misconceptionEqual rates do not mean equal concentrations. In most equilibria the concentrations are quite different (0.75 M vs 0.50 M in the example).

The law of mass action: Kc, Kp, and Q

What

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, at equilibrium:

Kc = [C]c[D]d / [A]a[B]b   (molar concentrations; solutions or gases)
Kp = (PC)c(PD)d / (PA)a(PB)b   (partial pressures in atm; gases)
Kp = Kc(RT)Δn   R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K, T in K, Δn = (c + d) − (a + b) for gases only
  • Homogeneous equilibrium: everything is in the same phase (all gases, or all aqueous).
  • Heterogeneous equilibrium: more than one phase. Pure solids and pure liquids are left out of K: PbS(s) + H2O(l) ⇌ Pb2+ + S2−, Kc = [Pb2+][S2−]; FeO(s) + CO(g) ⇌ Fe(s) + CO2(g), Kp = PCO2/PCO.
  • Size of K: K ≫ 1 favors products; K ≪ 1 favors reactants.
  • Kc = Kp only when Δn = 0, because (RT)0 = 1.

Why

Pure solids and liquids are left out because their "concentration" (density divided by molar mass) is fixed: adding more solid does not make it more concentrated. Their constant values are absorbed into K.

Kc and Kp differ because P = (n/V)RT = [X]RT for an ideal gas. Substituting [X]RT for every pressure leaves an extra factor of RT for each mole of gas, so the leftover is (RT)Δn.

Why constants multiply when reactions add (your past quiz question): ΔG° values of added reactions add, and ΔG° = −RT ln K. So −RT ln Ktotal = −RT(ln K1 + ln K2) = −RT ln(K1K2), giving Ktotal = K1 × K2. Reversing negates ΔG°, which turns ln K into −ln K, i.e. 1/K.

Le Châtelier's principle

What

Le Châtelier's principle (1884): if a system at equilibrium is subjected to a stress, the position of the equilibrium shifts in the direction that relieves the stress.

How

Treat heat as a reactant (endothermic) or a product (exothermic), then apply the same "add → shift away" logic.

Handout lab: the cobalt chloride equilibrium
[Co(H2O)6]2+ (pink) + 4Cl− + heat ⇌ [CoCl4]2− (blue) + 6H2O
  • Add HCl (more Cl−) → shifts right → blue.
  • Add water → shifts left → pink.
  • Heat (hot water bath) → shifts right → blue; ice bath → pink. So the forward reaction is endothermic: Reaction A in your handout (heat on the reactant side) matches the observed colors.
  • Add AgNO3 → AgCl precipitates, removing Cl− → shifts left → pink.

Why HCl, and can NaCl or KCl be used? All three supply the common ion Cl−, so in principle they push the equilibrium the same way. Concentrated HCl (about 12 M) delivers a very high [Cl−] in a few drops, giving a dramatic color change. Solid NaCl or KCl is limited by solubility (about 6 M at most) and dissolves slowly, so the shift is smaller and slower. H+ itself plays no part in this equilibrium.

Practical application: a humidity (moisture) indicator, such as cobalt chloride paper or the blue silica-gel beads in packaging, which turn pink when they absorb water.

Handout lab: chromate–dichromate
2CrO42− (yellow) + 2H+ ⇌ Cr2O72− (orange) + H2O

Add acid (H+) → shifts right → orange. Add base: OH− removes H+ → shifts left → yellow. It is the common-ion idea with H+.

Haber process: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3, ΔH = −92 kJ

High pressure favors NH3 (4 mol gas → 2). Low temperature favors NH3 (exothermic), but makes the reaction too slow, so industry compromises at about 400–450 °C with an iron catalyst and removes NH3 as it forms.

Why

Le Châtelier is a shortcut for comparing Q with K. Adding a reactant lowers Q below K, so the reaction runs forward until Q = K again. Squeezing a gas mixture changes every partial pressure by the same factor, but because the side with more gas moles has more pressure terms, Q changes and the system shifts toward fewer moles. Adding an inert gas at constant volume changes no partial pressure, so Q is untouched and nothing shifts.

Temperature is different: it is the only stress that changes K itself, because K = kf/kr and the endothermic direction's rate constant grows faster with temperature (larger activation energy). A catalyst lowers both activation energies equally, so it speeds both directions without moving the equilibrium.

"Good Houses Take Shape"

G = H − TS. Negative ΔG = "go." Use kelvin and match kJ with kJ.

Four cases

"Same signs, temperature decides"

Opposite signs: the answer never changes (−,+ always yes; +,− never). Same signs: crossover at T = ΔH/ΔS; negative ΔS → spontaneous at low T, positive ΔS → at high T.

Writing K

"Products over reactants; solids and liquids sit out"

Raise each concentration to its coefficient. Pure solids and liquids (including water as solvent) never appear in K.

Kc ↔ Kp

Kp = Kc(RT)Δn

Δn counts gas moles only, products minus reactants. R = 0.0821. If Δn = 0, they are equal.

Manipulating K

Flip → 1/K · ×n → Kn · Add → multiply

Because ΔG° = −RT ln K: flipping negates ΔG°, scaling scales it, and adding ΔG° values multiplies the Ks.

ΔG° and K

"Negative G, big K"

ΔG° < 0 ↔ K > 1 (products). ΔG° > 0 ↔ K < 1. About 5.7 kJ/mol of ΔG° = a factor of 10 in K at 25 °C.

Le Châtelier

"Push it, it pushes back"

Add → shift away. Remove → shift toward. Squeeze → fewer gas moles. Heat → endothermic side. Only temperature changes K.

Cobalt chloride

"Blue when hot and salty, pink when wet and cold"

[CoCl4]2− (blue) is favored by heat and Cl−; [Co(H2O)6]2+ (pink) by water and cooling. Used in humidity indicators.

Why does ΔH°rxn = ΣnΔH°f(products) − ΣmΔH°f(reactants)? Because you can take any reaction on a two-step detour: break the reactants into elements (undo their formation), then build the products from those elements. Elements in their standard states sit at zero.

9 · Analysis: Trouton's rule and hydrogen bonding

Lessons 9 and 11

At the normal boiling point, ΔGvap = 0, so ΔSvap = ΔHvap/Tb. Trouton noticed this ratio is about 88 J/mol·K for many liquids: the liquid-to-gas jump in disorder is roughly the same. The points that sit above the line are the ones whose liquids are more ordered than usual.
